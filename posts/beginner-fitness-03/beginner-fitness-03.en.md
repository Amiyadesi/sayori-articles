## Introduction

In the previous article we went through several common forms of aerobic exercise, and how to build an exercise habit from zero

This time it's strength training's turn

If running was what made the changes in my cardiorespiratory endurance and body weight most obvious, then the strength training I came to later made me start paying attention to another side of the body: muscle and strength

Walking into a gym for the first time, faced with all kinds of machines, dumbbells and barbells, you can end up not knowing where to start

Bench press, squat, deadlift, three-day split, five-day split, RM, failure...

Just looking at these terms is enough to make your head hurt, haha

But in fact, an ordinary person who wants to start strength training doesn't need to understand every piece of gym jargon first

This article starts from the most basic concepts, introduces some common exercises, and covers how to put together your first strength training plan

Even if you have no training experience at all, or don't even plan to go to a gym, you can still find a way to start that suits you

## 1. Why do strength training?

First we need to know what strength training actually is

Strength training, also called resistance training, simply means having muscles move against resistance, gradually increasing their strength and function

The resistance here can be dumbbells, barbells or gym machines, it can be resistance bands, or even your own body weight

Push-ups, for example, train muscle by working against part of your body weight

The benefits of strength training go beyond bigger muscles

It helps us:

- Improve muscle strength, so daily things like carrying bags or climbing stairs get easier
    
- Increase or maintain muscle mass and improve body composition
    
- Maintain bone health and physical function
    
- Combined with a reasonable diet, preserve as much muscle as possible while losing weight
    
- Improve overall physical capacity
    

In 2026 the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) published a new position stand on strength training, drawing together 137 systematic reviews and studies covering more than 30,000 participants

The research found that regular resistance training improves muscle strength, muscle mass, power and a number of measures of physical function

So whether or not you want visibly bigger muscles, strength training is worth considering as part of your exercise plan

**References:** [ACSM: 2026 position stand on strength training](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12965823/)

## 2. A few basic concepts before you start

If you've watched fitness videos or looked at training plans, you've probably seen descriptions like this:

**Dumbbell bench press: 3 sets × 10 reps**

What does that actually mean?

### 1. Reps and sets

**Rep (repetition):** one complete movement counts as one rep

For example, one push-up — lowering down and pushing back to the start — is one repetition

**Set:** several reps done in a row make one set

So "3 sets × 10 reps" means doing 10 reps, resting, then repeating that twice more, for 30 reps in total

How many reps each set should have is covered further on

### 2. Training weight

Strength training often requires choosing an appropriate weight

Too light, and it may not provide enough training stimulus

Too heavy, and the movement can get out of control, adding unnecessary risk

For beginners, I'd suggest starting with a light weight you can move with control, then increasing gradually based on how it actually goes

Another common term is **RM (repetition maximum)**

For example, 10RM usually means that under given conditions, the most you can do with that weight is 10 clean reps

But beginners absolutely do not need to test their one-rep maximum right at the start

### 3. Failure and reps in reserve

You may also have heard that "you have to train to failure for it to count"

Failure can simply be understood as: under the current conditions, you can no longer complete one more rep with acceptable form

But you don't have to reach failure every session

A very practical concept is **RIR (repetitions in reserve)** — roughly how many more reps you could still do

For example, if you finish 10 push-ups and feel that two more would be hard to keep clean, that's about RIR 2

For people just learning a movement, it's fine to leave a few reps in reserve and focus on controlling the movement and getting familiar with training

Once you have some experience, then think about how to adjust the intensity

### 4. Progressive overload

This is a very important principle in strength training

Simply put, once the body has adapted to the current training stimulus, you can appropriately increase the difficulty so the muscles keep getting new stimulus

For example:

- You could only do 8 reps at a certain weight, and later you can do 12
    
- You could only do wall push-ups, and later you can do incline push-ups
    
- You could only manage 1 set, and later you can do 2 sets reliably
    
Each of these counts as progress

That said, you don't need to add weight or reps every single session

**Progressing steadily matters more than rushing to break your own limits**

## 3. Which body parts should strength training cover?

The human body has many different muscles, and when you first encounter strength training it can feel like you need to memorise a lot of complicated muscle names

In fact we can start with a few categories of movement

### 1. Pushing movements: chest, shoulders and arms

Common exercises:

- Push-ups
    
- Dumbbell bench press
    
- Machine chest press
    
- Dumbbell shoulder press
    

These mainly involve the chest, shoulders and triceps, with the exact involvement depending on the movement

For beginners, push-ups are a good choice

If a standard push-up is too hard, start with wall push-ups, incline push-ups or knee push-ups

If you have gym access, you can also start with the machine chest press to learn the basic way of generating force

### 2. Pulling movements: back and arms

Common exercises:

- Resistance band row
    
- Dumbbell row
    
- Seated row
    
- Lat pulldown
    
- Pull-up
    

These train the back muscles, and also use the strength of the arms

Among them, pull-ups may be quite hard for someone with no training background at all

So there's no need to start with pull-ups

Band rows, machine rows and lat pulldowns are equally good choices for a beginner training their back

### 3. Lower-body movements: legs and glutes

Common exercises:

- Squat
    
- Chair squat
    
- Leg press
    
- Lunge
    
- Glute bridge
    
- Romanian deadlift
    

Leg training covers different movement patterns

For example, squats and the leg press mainly involve extension of the knee and hip, while glute bridges and Romanian deadlifts emphasise driving from the hips

With no training experience, you can start with chair squats and glute bridges

Once you're familiar with the movements, gradually add load

### 4. Core training

Common exercises:

- Plank
    
- Dead bug
    
- Bird dog
    
- Side plank
    

Core training is often misunderstood as simply training the abs

In fact the core involves multiple muscle groups around the abdomen, back and pelvis, and helps with trunk stability and control

For beginners, movements like the plank and dead bug are enough as a starting point

Of course, if your main goal is clearly visible abs, you also need to consider overall body fat level

Simply doing endless crunches won't reduce belly fat on its own in large amounts

Fat loss still happens across the whole body

## 5. How should a beginner arrange training?

### 1. How many times a week?

The World Health Organization recommends adults do muscle-strengthening activity involving major muscle groups on at least two days a week

For beginners, you can try two full-body sessions a week, with recovery time between them

For example:

|Day|Training|
|---|---|
|Monday|Full-body strength training|
|Tuesday|Brisk walk or rest|
|Wednesday|Aerobic exercise|
|Thursday|Full-body strength training|
|Friday|Rest|
|Saturday|Aerobic exercise or another activity you enjoy|
|Sunday|Free|

This is just an example — you can start according to your own habits

If you already run or have other exercise habits, you can slot strength training in among them

### 2. How many sets per exercise?

For someone with no experience at all, start with 2–3 sets per exercise and increase gradually

Many beginner programmes use 8–12 reps per set as a reference range, but that number isn't an absolute standard

Some bodyweight movements work with different rep counts, and some training is measured by duration instead — the plank, for example

When training, choose a difficulty where you can control the movement and that offers some challenge

As your body adapts, then consider adding sets or weight

### 3. How long to rest between sets?

Start by trying 1–2 minutes, and adjust according to intensity and how well you recover

If a movement leaves you clearly out of breath, or you can't complete the next set with control, rest a bit longer

There's no need to force your rest periods shorter just to chase a so-called training density

### 4. When should you add weight?

Say you're using 10 kg dumbbells for a movement and can only manage 8 reps per set

After a period of training, you can reliably do 12 reps, and feel you still have something left

That's when you can try adding a little weight

But remember: after increasing the weight, you should still be able to keep the movement controlled

## 6. Questions beginners often have

### 1. If I don't get sore after training, was it a waste?

Of course not

When you first start training, suddenly increase your volume or try a new movement, delayed onset muscle soreness is fairly common

It usually appears gradually from several hours to about a day after training, and may be more obvious over the following day or two

If you can gradually complete more reps, use a more suitable weight, or control the movement more skillfully, all of that is progress

Conversely, if you feel clear joint pain, sharp pain or discomfort that keeps getting worse during training, you should stop that movement and look into why

### 2. If I want to lose weight, do I still need strength training?

I'd recommend including a moderate amount of strength training

While managing body weight, we want to reduce excess fat as much as possible while maintaining muscle mass

Strength training provides the stimulus to maintain muscle, and combined with a reasonable diet and enough protein it helps improve body composition during weight loss

As for exactly how to eat and how much protein you need per day, we can leave that to a later nutrition article

### 3. Do you have to train to failure?

No

The 2026 ACSM evidence review found that whether training reaches momentary muscular failure does not consistently determine strength or muscle growth outcomes

That said, training still needs to provide enough stimulus — if you always use a weight that's too easy, progress may stall

For beginners, learning the movements well and then gradually increasing the challenge already gives you plenty to work on

### 4. Can bodyweight training really build muscle?

Yes

As long as the difficulty provides enough stimulus, bodyweight training can also improve muscle strength and, under the right conditions, promote muscle growth

Push-ups going from wall to incline to standard, for example, is one way of gradually raising the difficulty

As your strength improves, you can also continue with resistance bands, dumbbells or gym machines

Which form you choose mainly depends on your goals, interests and circumstances

### 5. Do you need to warm up before training? Should you stretch after?

Warming up appropriately is recommended

And ideally warm up the muscles you're about to train, with the movements you're about to use — that helps the body get into the training state better

For example, before training chest you can do some dynamic shoulder work first, then warm up with lighter bench press sets

Before training legs you can do bodyweight squats and work the hips and ankles, then gradually increase the training weight

This preparation helps the body adapt to the exercise that follows, especially when the training weight keeps going up

During training, don't hold your breath and strain for long periods either — beginners especially should keep a natural, controlled breathing rhythm

As for stretching afterwards, I'd also recommend arranging it appropriately according to your own needs

You can do gentle static stretching for the muscles you've just trained, holding each for about 15–30 seconds, without chasing any feeling of pain

Static stretching can help improve flexibility and range of motion, and if your body feels better after stretching, it's absolutely fine to keep it as a habit

## 7. Learning resources worth following

Strength training involves a great many movements and techniques, and one article obviously can't cover them all

I've already recommended some teaching videos suited to beginners; here are a few sites worth consulting long-term

If you come across a movement you don't recognise later, or want to go deeper into exercise science, you can come back to these

### 1. MuscleWiki

[The MuscleWiki exercise database](https://musclewiki.com/exercises)

You can look up exercises by body part or equipment, and the site also provides demonstrations

For beginners who've just encountered a gym and don't even know the machines' names, it's fairly convenient

### 2. NHS Strength Exercises

[The NHS home strength training guide](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/exercise/strength-exercises/)

A basic training guide published by the UK National Health Service, including low-barrier movements like chair stands and wall push-ups

It suits people with no training background at all who want to try something at home first

### 3. American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM)

[The official ACSM website](https://acsm.org/)

This is a professional organisation dedicated to researching and promoting knowledge in sports medicine and exercise science

If you're interested in strength training research, training methods and health-related knowledge, you can read further in its public materials

### 4. E3 Rehab

[E3 Rehab resources](https://e3rehab.com/media/)

It mainly covers rehabilitation, pain, strength training and physical function

For people who want to understand joint load, sports injuries and rehab training better, it works as further reading

Of course, whether it's a website or a fitness creator, there's no need to treat every single thing they say as an absolutely correct conclusion

When you hit a specific question, it's best to combine several reliable sources with your own situation

## 8. Final thoughts

Many people encountering strength training for the first time don't dare go into a gym, feeling that everyone inside is already impressive

But everyone has their own starting point — at the beginning we're all pretty much the same, so there's nothing to be afraid of

Gyms do have more and more specialised equipment, and if you can get to one it's certainly worth trying, to find the way of training that suits you

If you can't even do one standard push-up right now, you can start with knee, incline or wall push-ups

If you've never touched a barbell, you can learn bodyweight movements first or use fixed machines

If you can't get to a gym for now, starting at home is completely fine too

Another thing I find really interesting about strength training is that its progress can often be recorded

The first standard push-up, the first time lifting a dumbbell you used to think was heavy, or noticing that carrying things day to day has become easier

These changes may be small, but added up they let you feel your body slowly changing

I hope this article helps you understand strength training, and makes you willing to give it a try yourself

Next time, I plan to keep talking about diet and recovery

After all, exercise is only part of the whole process — what you eat, how you eat, and how you arrange sleep and rest are just as worth understanding properly
