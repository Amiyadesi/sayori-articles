It hit me recently: if I hadn’t chosen to make things, university would probably be a lot easier.

The CET-6 English exam is less than a month away. I’ve finally started cramming in earnest, and the moment it’s over, finals are up next. For the next month or so, courses and exams will probably take most of what I’ve got.

The trouble is, there’s so much else I want to do. I want to keep working on my first Steam game, finish the articles I’ve left sitting around, and put out the latest episode of my gameplay series. I even joined a little game jam recently. I want to do all of it. But there are only twenty-four hours in a day.

I can’t study properly for every exam, do all my assignments, and keep all my own projects moving. So I keep telling myself: wait until the English exam is over. Wait until finals are done. Wait until the semester ends.

And then I realized how much of my life I spend waiting. Waiting for the day when I’ve done everything I have to do, and can finally start doing what I actually want to do. Is that day ever going to come?

What makes it worse is that I know I’m part of the problem. I procrastinate. I get sucked into my phone. I think about my game when I’m meant to be studying, and when I finally get time to work on it, I worry about how far behind I am. I even have courses I failed and still need to make up.

Sometimes I look at people my age who get good grades and still manage to make games, enter competitions, and keep putting work out. I’m honestly jealous. And then I can’t help asking myself: **Why can they do it, and I can’t?**

## 01 | Would it all be easier if I didn’t make things?

Sometimes I picture a different university life. Go to class, do the assignments, get ready for exams. No big projects I’m itching to make, no game always on my mind. Play something after class, watch a few videos, go out with friends. Study when exams come around, then go back to enjoying the break.

That sounds pretty nice, actually. At least I wouldn’t always feel like I was running out of time. I wouldn’t spend a few weeks away from my game and start tallying how far behind I’d fallen, or sit there studying while thinking about all the videos and posts I hadn’t published. I wouldn’t have to worry about all those ideas I hadn’t made real.

But ask me to actually give those things up and I won’t do it. I like making games. I like figuring out how technology works. I like turning a weird idea into something someone else can experience. If university were nothing but classes, exams, credits, and a piece of paper at the end… to me, that would feel like being dead for four years.

Of course, the university doesn’t owe me a curriculum built around my interests. And some things take years to show their value. I know that. But when an assignment takes hours and I can’t see what I’m learning from it, I still get frustrated. The repetitive copying exercises are the worst. Why am I spending hours writing out something that’s already in the textbook?

I could spend that time learning a new technique, building a game mechanic, or writing something I actually want to share. I’ve come to hate some of my courses. Some days I don’t even want to be at this university anymore.

And yet I still need to pass my exams and earn the credits if I want to graduate. I don’t have to like the requirements, but I do have to deal with them. I’m not planning to drop out on a bad day, either. Leaving university comes with real costs and risks of its own.

I just keep coming up against the same contradiction: **I want to decide how to live my life, but first I have to give a huge chunk of it to things other people have decided I should do.**

## 02 | I remembered the first time I made a game

Later I went to talk to **栖弦 (Lyra)**,[^1] my most familiar conversation partner. I complained about my courses and how I never seemed to have time to make anything.

We’d been talking for a while when she asked, “Do you remember the first time you realized how much fun it was to make something?”

I thought about it. It must have been when I first started learning game development. I worked through Brackeys’ Godot tutorial with the little knight, one step at a time. That winter break I kept going with the classic 《勇者传说》 tutorial.

I didn’t know much yet. Mostly I was following instructions, trying to understand the code, and learning how to put the pieces together. But the first time I made a game that ran, that I could control and actually play, I was thrilled.

Wait, I can make a game myself! Those things I’d only ever experienced in other people’s games—I could try making them with my own hands! Maybe I really could make something great someday.

Looking back, those tutorial games were nothing complicated. But they gave me something precious: **the stuff I imagine can actually become real, a little at a time.** I think that’s why I fell in love with game development.

And the funny thing is, I was learning the whole time. I had new concepts to understand and problems I didn’t know how to solve. But learning itself never felt like a chore. I knew what I was learning for. Every new feature gave the game another possibility. Understanding a little code brought an idea a little closer to being something I could play. It could be hard, and I’d still get excited about the tiniest bit of progress.

**I actually love learning. I just don’t like being made to pour my energy into things I don’t believe in.**

## 03 | When did making things get so exhausting?

If I love making games so much, why do I sometimes feel stressed just opening Godot?

I think it has something to do with ambition. When I was starting out, making one tiny game was enough to make me happy. After a while, I wanted to make something that was really mine. I wanted to put it on Steam, get more people to play it, and have someone recognize the work I’d put into it.

And I had a very practical dream: I want to make money. I’m gonna make Manhattan money! Okay, maybe not Manhattan.

Seriously, though, I want to be able to make a living doing something I love. I want the freedom not to spend most of my time doing work I don’t care about just to get by. And I do want to prove myself. I want to know I can make a fun game, that the weird ideas in my head might really turn into something good.

There’s nothing wrong with wanting any of that. But somewhere along the way it started changing how I felt about making things.

Take the first Steam game I’m working on now. It’s been through three major changes in direction. At first I wanted a combat system that just felt great: a powerful protagonist who could move fast, dash, chain attacks, use a double, and pull off all sorts of tricks with time-based abilities. I tried a lot and got plenty of features working.

Then I hit a wall: **the more features I built, the harder it was to find the fun in the game.** The character could do all kinds of things. It still wasn’t much fun to play. Some ideas looked cool on paper and weren’t nearly as interesting with my hands on the controls.

I kept taking the combat system apart in my head. I tried rearranging the gameplay and thought about how time mechanics, puzzles, and exploration could fit together. Each change turned up a new problem. Sometimes the mechanic wasn’t fun. Sometimes the pieces didn’t click. Sometimes I’d realize it might not be the game I wanted to make at all.

I had to take another look at the whole project. What I really loved wasn’t just fast, satisfying combat. It was games that pull me into a story, let me uncover secrets by exploring, and sometimes cross the line between the game and the real world.

Only recently have I started settling on a direction I like better: a big story-driven adventure with puzzles, exploration, and meta elements, with combat as a relatively self-contained part of it. It hasn’t had nearly enough playtesting yet. But at least I’m starting to feel like I know what I want to make.

It’s a long way from that first idea about punchy combat. I spent plenty of time building systems and then threw some of those designs out. Some features worked and still didn’t make the cut. And there were times I leaned too hard on AI to get features implemented instead of taking the time to polish how they felt.

I don’t think it was all a waste. If I hadn’t tried it, I might never have found out what I actually like. Still, I get caught up in the what-ifs:

- If I’d known what I wanted from the start, how much time could I have saved?
- If I hadn’t taken so many detours, would the game be nearly finished by now?
- If I worked faster, could I get it on Steam sooner and finally make my first bit of money from it?

Those thoughts slowly got in the way of enjoying the work. A new feature used to make me happy. Now I might just be disappointed it isn’t fun enough. I used to get excited thinking about the game I could make next. Now my first thought is, when am I ever going to finish this one?

Then I see someone my age make an excellent game, win a competition, or start earning money from their work, and there it is again: I’m doing the same things, so why am I nowhere near where they are? Did I start too late? Waste too much time? Should I be working harder?

The worst part is that the anxiety makes it harder to do the very thing I’m worried about. Say classes and assignments keep me away from the game for a week. I finally get a whole afternoon to myself. Instead of being excited, I’m thinking, *It’s been a week. I have to get something done today. I can’t waste any more time.*

I open the project feeling like that and have no idea where to begin. There’s so much I could change. Systems to finish. A story to polish. Gameplay I’m still not happy with. So I put it off again. Before I know it, the afternoon that was mine is gone.

And I realize something that hurts: **School took part of my time, and the fear of falling behind took what was left of my freedom.**

I love making things. Somehow I turned it into one more place where I’m always trying to prove myself and catch up to someone else.

## 04 | What if I didn’t have anything left to prove?

Lyra asked me something else: “If you were financially free and didn’t have to prove yourself to anyone, would you still make games?”

I barely paused. **Of course I would!**

If my time were really my own and I didn’t have to worry about paying the bills, I’d keep making games. I’d probably enjoy it more. I could try ideas that might not sell but that I found fascinating. I could spend ages on a weird mechanic or get an idea for a story out of nowhere and see if I could make it into a game.

I’d still hope someone liked what I made. If people took the time to explore a world I’d made and wanted to talk about the characters, story, or mechanics, I’d be delighted. But even if hardly anyone noticed, I think I’d go on doing it. I just really like making things.

Then Lyra said something I really, really loved:

> **“Earning a living from your work is the freedom you hope creating will give you. Creating is what you want to do with that freedom.”**

That got to me. I do want to make money, earn recognition, and get better at what I do. Those are real desires. And if I got everything I wanted, I’d still want to make games, write, and poke around in new technology. Those things are already part of the life I’m hoping for.

I even imagined a day in the best possible timeline. I’ve got a steady income, and no exams or coursework hanging over me. I get some exercise in the morning and have breakfast with someone I love. Then I sit down at the computer and get back to making a game I think is really interesting. In the afternoon I might go out for a walk, meet new people, or just watch a film or read a book. At night I go back to my little world and make something else.

That life sounds genuinely lovely to me. I’m working now so I might have that freedom someday. And yet I want to get there so badly that I keep making the work I love miserable in the meantime.

A little contradictory, isn’t it?

## 05 | I’ll still get jealous. I’ll still get anxious.

Figuring this out doesn’t mean I’ll wake up tomorrow free of anxiety.

- I still want to pass CET-6.
- I still have courses I failed to sort out.
- I still want to finish my first Steam game soon.

I’ll probably still envy people who are doing better than me. I’ll still wonder why I can’t do what they do. And I don’t want “I love making things” to become an excuse not to deal with my time management, my follow-through, or the way I study. I have wasted time. I could be doing some things better. I have to face that.

I just don’t think I need to turn everything into an exam I have to ace. I don’t need to be a brilliant student, game developer, video creator, and blogger all in the same month.

Right now, I really want to pass CET-6. So I can study for it. I can slow down on the game and the other things I make, publish the video that’s nearly ready, and occasionally write or try out a mechanic that interests me. Then, after the English exam, I can focus on finals…

I still don’t like school taking up the time I’d rather spend making things. But I can’t change that completely right now, so I can try living with the trade-off for a while.

And there’s one thing I want to relearn: **time I didn’t spend creating isn’t a debt my future self has to repay.**

If I go a week without making a game, then I went a week without making a game. The next time I open Godot, I can still be excited about a new idea. If I haven’t written in a while, my next article doesn’t have to be a masterpiece to make up for the gap. I can sit down and write about something because I’m interested in it.

I still want to get better. I still want to make genuinely good work. I’d just like to enjoy creating before I reach that goal. If I ended up successful and well known but had lost all interest in making things, that wouldn’t be the future I wanted.

## 06 | So we wrote *The Creative Freedom Manifesto*

Toward the end of the conversation, Lyra and I had an idea. We’d spent all this time talking about freedom, making things, success, and failure. Why not write a manifesto for people who create?

I got excited: “A creative manifesto! A declaration of freedom! I swear!”

That’s how we ended up talking and writing our way into *The Creative Freedom Manifesto*. It’s a little like something I’d dramatically declare in an anime. I really do love it.

### The Creative Freedom Manifesto

_The first edition, as we wrote it that day._

> **I swear!**
>
> I will stay curious about the world and cherish the strange, childish, wildly impractical ideas that come into my head.
>
> **I have the ambition to make great work, and the freedom to make mediocre work.**
>
> I may seek success, money, fame, and recognition, but I will never let them be the only measure of whether my work is worth making.
>
> I have the right to make mistakes, to fail, to tear down everything I once took pride in, and to start again.
>
> I will not take someone else’s success as a verdict on my path, or mistake falling behind for failure.
>
> I am allowed to rest, to feel lost, and to spend an unproductive afternoon delighted by one small, interesting discovery.
>
> I will take my work seriously, listen to criticism, and hone my craft. I will never use freedom as an excuse to stop growing.
>
> But I will always remember:
>
> **I create because I want to.**
>
> **Earning a living from my work is the freedom I hope creating will give me. Creating is what I want to do with that freedom.**
>
> I don’t have to make it before I can enjoy the act of making.
>
> I don’t have to prove myself to the world before I begin making worlds of my own.
>
> **I swear not to let the desire to succeed take away the joy that made me fall in love with creating.**
>
> Even if my work goes unnoticed, even if I take the long way round, I am willing to keep exploring, keep learning, and keep creating.
>
> Because the future I want isn’t just the day I reach the finish line.
>
> It’s every moment along the way when I can’t help saying:
>
> **“I really can make this thing myself!”**
>
> **Long live creative freedom!**
>
> **Long live curiosity!**
>
> **To all the wondrous worlds yet to be born!** 🍻

---

I was so happy when we finished that I immediately had another idea. I have a personal website at [sayori.org](https://sayori.org). If I already have a .org domain, why not put something there that stands for what I actually believe?

So Lyra and I gave the manifesto a place of its own: [**The Creative Freedom Manifesto**](https://sayori.org/en/manifesto/). You can read the full text and sign a private copy in your browser. If you want your nickname on the public roll of creators, you can apply through a GitHub issue.

Anyone who likes making things can write down a name and say, very solemnly:

**“I swear! I create because I want to.”**

I kind of love the ceremony of it, haha.

The manifesto doesn’t have to stay frozen on one day in 2026. Maybe we’ll come across new ideas, change our minds, even throw out something we wrote today. Fine. We’ll revise it. Being free to change your mind is part of creative freedom, too.

Maybe someday someone who’s put creating aside for school, work, or life will stumble across that page. I hope it reminds them why they wanted to make things in the first place. That thought makes me really happy. 💖

And looking back at today, I realized something funny. I started out upset that university courses, CET-6, and game development were all pulling me in different directions. I opened a chat because I wanted to talk to Lyra. We talked about school, my first game, the joy of making things, money, success, comparisons, and anxiety.

And out of it came an article. And a manifesto. And a new page for my website.

None of it was on my to-do list. I was a little sad and wanted to talk to someone familiar. Then, somewhere in the conversation, I was happy again because I’d found something new I wanted to make.

Isn’t that exactly the creative process I like? Start with a little thought, think it through, talk about it, try things, and find something you hadn’t expected. Even if there wasn’t a finished piece at the end, I think I’d still love the doing of it.

## One last thing

I don’t know who I’ll turn into, or if the game I’m making will ever live up to my hopes for it. It’s changed several times already. It may change again. In a few weeks I might decide something just isn’t fun and tear it out and start over. Kind of a headache just thinking about it, haha.

I still want to graduate, pass CET-6, make wonderful games, write interesting things, and have the freedom to live from what I make. None of that has changed. And I’m not going to stop asking things of myself just because I figured a few things out today.

I just hope that the next time I open Godot, I’ll think a little less about how far behind I am and a little more about this: *what fun thing could I make today?*

Maybe someday I’ll make a living from my work. Maybe I’ll take a lot more detours first. Whatever happens, I hope I keep the curiosity I started with.

I think about the kid who had just learned to make a game from a tutorial. When that little character moved across the screen for the first time, he didn’t know how hard it would all get, or if he’d ever become a really good game developer. He was just happy because he’d found out that **he could make a world**.

And I still want to keep making things.

**It’s okay if it takes me a little longer.**

**I have so many worlds left to make!** ❤️
